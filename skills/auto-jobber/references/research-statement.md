# The statement shows how the applicant thinks

## What a research statement must achieve

A research statement shows reviewers how the applicant thinks as a researcher. Reviewers read it for the applicant's problem formulation, the development of their research over time, their ability to propose meaningful future directions and their fit with the research group's themes. Explaining a few problems in depth therefore serves the statement better than an exhaustive summary of the applicant's work.

A statement of purpose or personal statement shares the name but asks why the applicant wants the position, so it is a motivation letter: follow [Voice](voice.md) for it, and this reference for any research parts it requests.

## Follow the group's own guidance first

Treat the posting, the group's website and any statement guide they link as the offer, so Voice's rule on the offer's authority governs the statement. Their page limit, sections, themes and requested artifacts override the defaults below. Read the guide in full before planning and record its requirements in the application records with exact quotations. Follow [Research](research.md) for every claim about the group, its themes or its members' publications.

## Default structure

Without other guidance, write two to three pages in three sections:

| Section | Length | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Research background | 0.5–1 page | Show what the applicant has worked on and learned as a researcher |
| Research vision | 1–1.5 pages | Set out the problems the applicant wants to investigate next and how to begin |
| Fit to the group | about 0.5 page | Show where that vision meets the group's themes |

The vision is the most important section, so it gets the most space and the closest review.

## Research background

Explain a few contributions well instead of listing many projects. For each selected contribution, state the research problem, the applicant's specific contribution, the methods or systems they used or developed, and what they learned scientifically. Select the contributions that lead into the vision, because the background shows where the applicant's questions come from.

Take problems, roles and methods from the factual records. What the applicant learned is their own interpretation, so obtain it from them rather than inferring a lesson from the facts.

## Research vision

Develop one or two clearly defined research threads. For each, address:

- which problem the applicant finds important, and why;
- which critical limitation of current approaches it exposes;
- the applicant's ideas, explicit questions or hypotheses;
- how they would practically begin to investigate them.

The section needs specific scientific direction, not a complete research proposal or an exhaustive literature survey. "I am interested in AI safety and LLMs" names a field without a question, so it gives no direction.

## Connect the vision to submitted artifacts

When the application includes artifacts, such as publications, preprints, repositories or technical reports, connect the vision to them explicitly: name the artifact, the limitation the applicant met in it and the direction that limitation motivates. Cite only limitations that the artifact or the applicant's records document, and check each artifact's public text before citing it. Help the applicant choose the artifacts that best support the threads, within the number the group allows.

## Fit to the group

Connect the vision to the group's named themes in the group's own terms. Show the bridge from the applicant's prior work, perspective or skills, and say whether the vision would deepen one of the group's directions, open a complementary line of inquiry or start a collaboration. One well-reasoned connection is enough; the statement need not cover every theme. Link a group member's publication only where it addresses the same problem as the applicant's thread, and attribute to it only what it states.

Apply Voice's substitution test to this section: if changing the group's name and themes leaves it equally convincing, the connection is too generic.

## Get the applicant's thinking first

Reviewers read the statement as evidence of the applicant's independent thinking, so its research ideas must be the applicant's. Before drafting, collect their existing wording from the profile, Entry notes, earlier letters and artifacts, and check it against the background and vision requirements. Ask targeted questions for what is missing, for example:

- "What did this project teach you scientifically?"
- "Which limitation of current methods matters most to you, and why?"
- "What would you test or measure first, and how would you begin?"

The agent may find threads in the applicant's wording, phrase the applicant's own problem as an explicit question, point to a limitation their artifact documents and propose an order. It must not supply the problems, hypotheses, lessons or first steps itself. Where an answer is still missing, leave a visible gap in the draft and label the draft incomplete. If the applicant has no research question yet, follow [Drafting](drafting.md): the statement names the group's open problems they want to work on, in the group's words. Keep hypotheses and hopes recognisable as such; a planned test is never a result.

## Keep the applicant's voice

Write in the applicant's register, as Voice requires for letters, using clear scientific prose built from problems, claims, evidence and questions. Use a personal detail or Personality entry only where it explains a research question, because a line that suits an industry letter can read differently to a research group.

## Trace every sentence

Keep a provenance record in the application records, outside the draft file and the conversation, giving each sentence a label and an exact source locator:

| Label | Source |
| --- | --- |
| A | Applicant wording, adjusted only for tense or grammar |
| F | Checked factual record |
| R | Artifact's public text |
| L | Offer, guide or group quotation |
| B | Agent-written link, question or formulation |

Review every B sentence with the applicant before anything else, since those sentences put words into the applicant's research reasoning. Note material left out on purpose, with the reason, so a later revision does not reintroduce it unchecked.

## Review before calling the statement complete

Point to the passages that meet each section's requirements, then check the draft against the common weaknesses:

- the CV rewritten in paragraph form;
- projects listed without the applicant's role or insight;
- overly broad statements of interest;
- many topics without depth;
- past work without future direction.

Check that each thread has at least one explicit question or hypothesis and a practical first step from the applicant, or, where the applicant has none yet, the group's open problems they chose, that each artifact link matches the artifact, and that every claim about the group matches its source. Render the statement and confirm its page count within the group's limit, following Tailoring's rendering and inspection checks. A statement with open gaps or unreviewed B sentences remains a working draft; only the applicant can approve it.
