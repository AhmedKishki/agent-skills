# Teaching

## Lessons

Teach one lesson per turn. Start from what the level assessment says the student holds, and build each idea on the one before it.

- **Motivate before you define.** Say what problem the idea solves or why anyone needed it, because an idea that answers a question the student already feels is easier to accept than one that arrives from nowhere.
- **Make every abstract claim concrete** with an example. Where the topic has procedures, work one through.
- **Define each term** where it is first used. When the lesson needs a term or result from an earlier lesson, restate it in a sentence instead of pointing back, because the student rereads each lesson note on its own.
- **Teach an argument by its structure** when the material argues for something, as a philosophical text or a paper's thesis does: the question it answers, the claim, its grounds, the steps from grounds to claim and the limits of the conclusion. Keep the source's qualifications, since dropping one changes what the source claims.
- **Cite a source for every claim**, as [sources.md](sources.md) describes.
- **Use diagrams, tables or code** where they show something prose can't. Mermaid and ASCII both work.
- **Don't narrate the structure** ("first I will explain X, then Y") or summarize what you just said. The student's attention should go to the material.

Write the lesson note as [records.md](records.md) describes, deliver the lesson, ask whether the student has questions, and end your turn.

Answer each question the student asks, with references, then ask whether there are more. A question that belongs to a later lesson gets a short answer and the lesson number that covers it. If a question shows that a prerequisite is missing, propose a syllabus change as [planning.md](planning.md) describes. When the student has no more questions, go to the check.

## Check

Pose 1–5 questions, one for each key idea of the lesson, mixing multiple-choice and free-thought as [questions.md](questions.md) describes. A good check question:

- can be answered from what the lesson taught and what the student already held;
- makes the student use the idea on a new case (predict, apply, compare, find the error) instead of repeating the lesson's sentences, because a student can repeat words they haven't understood;
- has multiple-choice distractors that each match a specific wrong idea, so a wrong pick tells you what to re-explain.

Grade each answer as correct or incorrect and give the reason in a line, with its reference. Judge free-thought answers by their reasoning, not their wording. A slip, such as an arithmetic error inside sound reasoning, counts as correct; point it out.

- **All correct:** mark the lesson `done`, name the next lesson and start it when the student says so.
- **Any incorrect:** go to re-explanation.

## Re-explanation

Re-explain only the ideas the student missed.

- **Start from the wrong answer.** It shows the student's model: name that model plainly and show the case where it breaks.
- **Come at the idea from a new angle**, such as a different example, an analogy, smaller steps or a direct contrast with the wrong model. The first explanation already failed for this student, so repeating it rarely helps. A second source that explains the idea differently can supply the new angle.
- **Check again with new questions** on the missed ideas. The student now knows the answers to the old questions, so those can no longer show understanding.

If the student misses the same idea after two re-explanations, the syllabus has probably assumed a prerequisite they lack. Ask a question or two to find it, then propose a lesson for it as [planning.md](planning.md) describes.

## Completion

When the last lesson is done, mark the syllabus `complete` and tell the student. Offer a final check that mixes questions from across the course, graded and re-explained like any other check.
